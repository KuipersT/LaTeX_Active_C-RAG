\section{Active C-RAG}\label{sec:approach}

In this section we describe the implementation of the schema given in Section~\ref{sec:prob_form}. We begin by discussing how the state-of-the-art instantiates this schema, and then provide our specific implementation for each of system components in Figure~\ref{fig:system}.

\subsection{Prior Art}\label{subsec:prior_schema}

In the schema of Figure~\ref{fig:system}, CF and C-RAG only use round $0$. CF performs no retrieval, so that $D_0 = \emptyset$, the LLM $G$ writes the claim $y$ from the query $x$ alone, and the subclaim scorer returns the confidence score $s(c \mid x)$ of the model. C-RAG instead retrieves $D_0$ with the query $x$, and its subclaim scorer returns the score $s(c \mid x, D_0)$ as in \eqref{eq:crag_score}. Multi-hop C-RAG~\cite{rehman2026conformal} runs the initial retrieval over several hops, in which each query is rewritten by an LLM from $x$ and the evidence found in earlier hops, and keeps the score \eqref{eq:crag_score}. In all three methods, the decision block keeps every subclaim, i.e., $k_0 = \hat{y}_0$ and $w_0 = z_0 = \emptyset$, the system stops at $t^* = 0$, and the returned set is $\hat{y} = F_{\hat{\tau}}(\hat{y}_0)$.

Active RAG methods also use the rounds $t \geq 1$. FLARE~\cite{jiang2023active} retrieves whenever the next sentence of the answer contains low-confidence tokens and regenerates that sentence, and RARR~\cite{gao2023rarr} retrieves evidence for a finished answer and revises the parts that the evidence does not support. In terms of Figure~\ref{fig:system}, these methods decide heuristically which parts of the answer to rework, revise sentences rather than subclaims, and have no calibrated final filtering step, and thus provide no guarantee of the form \eqref{eq:cf}.

In the conformal methods, $t^* = 0$, so that a supported subclaim for which $D_0$ contains no supporting document, such as $c^{\star}_2$ in the example of Section~\ref{sec:intro}\tk{note still reworking the intro}, receives a low score and is removed by $F_{\hat{\tau}}$. Active RAG methods can retrieve the missing evidence, but without a guarantee. AC-RAG combines the rework rounds of active RAG with the calibrated decisions and final filtering step of CF and C-RAG.

\subsection{Base Pipeline}\label{subsec:base}

AC-RAG uses round $0$ of (multi-hop) C-RAG, unchanged, i.e., the initial retrieval, the LLM $G$ and the subclaim extraction, so that with $T = 0$, the system differs from these methods only in the subclaim scorer. The subclaims of $\hat{y}_0$ are kept in the order $c_1, c_2, \ldots$ in which they appear in $y$, as an answer typically states a bridge subclaim, such as $c_1$, before the subclaims that rely on it. \tk{The ordering of the subclaims may be kind of important, so it's worth raising this now in case we need to discuss the ordering further.}

\subsection{Subclaim Scorer}\label{subsec:scorer}

In practice, the retrieved documents are short passages, such as chunks of an article or single sentences, and we refer to them as passages. The score \eqref{eq:crag_score} has two key issues when the answer to a query relies on a bridge entity. Firstly, the cosine similarity $\mathrm{sim}(c, d)$ measures how close a subclaim and a passage are in topic, not whether the passage supports the subclaim. Secondly, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:intro}, the factor $\mathrm{sim}(x, d)$ penalises passages about the bridge entity, as these are not very similar to the query. Hence, the score of $c^{\star}_2$ would remain low even if Spielberg's biography were retrieved. To score whether the evidence supports a subclaim, we therefore replace \eqref{eq:crag_score} by a natural language inference (NLI) model. For a passage $d$ and a subclaim $c$, the NLI model returns the probabilities $p_{\mathrm{ent}}(d, c)$, $p_{\mathrm{con}}(d, c)$ and $p_{\mathrm{neu}}(d, c)$ that $d$ entails $c$, contradicts $c$, or neither, which sum to $1$. The scorer then assigns two scores to each subclaim $c \in \hat{y}_t$,
\begin{equation}
  s^{\mathrm{ent}}_t(c \mid x, D_t) = \max_{d \in D_t} p_{\mathrm{ent}}(d, c), \qquad s^{\mathrm{con}}_t(c \mid x, D_t) = \max_{d \in D_t} p_{\mathrm{con}}(d, c),
  \label{eq:nli_score}
\end{equation}
such that $s_t$ now contains several scores for each subclaim. The entailment score $s^{\mathrm{ent}}_t$ is high only if some passage entails $c$, and it does not depend on how the query $x$ is worded. Spielberg's biography, for instance, clearly entails $c^{\star}_2$, although it is not similar to $x$. The contradiction score $s^{\mathrm{con}}_t$ is high if some passage contradicts $c$, and it is only used by the decision block to remove subclaims. Entailment-based scorers have also been found to match or outperform LLM confidence scores for conformal factuality in RAG, at a far lower computational cost \cite{chen2026conformal}.

The scorer also returns the evidence that each round adds for each subclaim. For a subclaim $c$, let $N_t(c) \subseteq D_t$ denote the passages of $D_t$ that have not been scored against $c$ in earlier rounds. The new evidence for $c$ at round $t$ is then the best of these passages,
\begin{equation}
  p_t(c) = \max_{d \in N_t(c)} p_{\mathrm{ent}}(d, c),
  \label{eq:new_score}
\end{equation}
which is only defined when $N_t(c) \neq \emptyset$. A passage therefore contributes to $p_t(c)$ at most once for each subclaim, including a passage that was retrieved for a different subclaim. \tk{I think this is how we should treat this. Every round is a new bet against the new evidence that is retrieved so that the e-process only considers a piece of evidence once. Does this make sense?}

\subsection{Decision Block}\label{subsec:decision}

\tk{Why not e-scores: they compare a subclaim's score with those of false subclaims from the pilot set, computed once at round $0$. It is therefore only valid for a single comparison at round $0$. After rework, the subclaim is scored on evidence retrieved specifically for it, so the comparison no longer holds, and checking it again at every round inflates the error. Calibrating a fresh e-score on a pilot set at each round wouldn't resolve this either, since multiplying them requires each one to be valid given the history of the query, whereas a conformal e-score is only valid on average over queries. So instead we can use betting, in which each round is a new bet that is fair given everything observed before, and the bets are multiplied into an e-process.}

Taking the null hypothesis $H_c$ for each subclaim $c$ to be that $c$ is unsupported, i.e., $a(c \mid x) = 0$, accepting $c$ as supported is equivalent to rejecting $H_c$, which we call certifying $c$. The guarantee \eqref{eq:cf_seq} therefore bounds the family-wise error rate (FWER) over the subclaims of a single answer, i.e., the probability of certifying at least one unsupported subclaim. Since the decision block is applied at every round, and the revision creates new subclaims from the evidence of earlier rounds, both the hypotheses and the number of tests depend on the data.

The decision block first removes the subclaims that the evidence contradicts. To this end, a subclaim that is not certified is removed when at least one passage contradicts it and no passage entails it, which, in terms of the scores \eqref{eq:nli_score}, gives
\begin{equation}
  z_t = \bigl\{ c \in \hat{y}_t \setminus k_{t-1} : s^{\mathrm{con}}_t(c \mid x, D_t) > 1/2 \ \text{and} \ s^{\mathrm{ent}}_t(c \mid x, D_t) \leq 1/2 \bigr\},
  \label{eq:removal}
\end{equation}
with $k_{t-1} = \emptyset$ for $t = 0$. Since the three probabilities of the NLI model sum to $1$, a probability above $1/2$ is the largest of the three, so that a passage with $p_{\mathrm{con}}(d, c) > 1/2$ contradicts $c$, and $s^{\mathrm{ent}}_t(c \mid x, D_t) \leq 1/2$ means that no passage entails $c$ with a probability above $1/2$. We note that removal requires no calibration, as a removed subclaim is never certified, and thus cannot lead to an error in \eqref{eq:cf_seq}.

For the remaining subclaims, the decision block accumulates evidence over the rounds with an e-process \cite{ramdas2023game}. Its validity rests on a bound on how high the new evidence for an unsupported subclaim scores. Specifically, we require a constant $\bar{p}_{\mathrm{null}} \in (0, 1)$ such that, for every subclaim $c$ with $a(c \mid x) = 0$ and every round $t$ at which $p_t(c)$ is defined,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}\bigl[\, p_t(c) \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1} \,\bigr] \leq \bar{p}_{\mathrm{null}},
  \label{eq:null_bound}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$ denotes everything observed up to round $t-1$. In other words, $\bar{p}_{\mathrm{null}}$ is the level up to which, on average, the NLI model is fooled by the new evidence for an unsupported subclaim. For each subclaim $c$, the e-process $M_t(c)$ starts at $1$ at the round at which $c$ is created, and at every round at which $p_t(c)$ is defined, it is updated as
\begin{equation}
  M_t(c) = M_{t-1}(c) \bigl(1 + \lambda_t(c) \bigl(p_t(c) - \bar{p}_{\mathrm{null}}\bigr)\bigr),
  \label{eq:betting}
\end{equation}
while it is left unchanged at the other rounds. The stake $\lambda_t(c) \in [0, 1/\bar{p}_{\mathrm{null}}]$ is chosen from $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$, for instance as a constant or adaptively as in \cite{waudbysmith2023betting}, and its upper limit keeps $M_t(c)$ non-negative. Under \eqref{eq:null_bound}, each factor in \eqref{eq:betting} has expectation at most $1$ given $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$ for an unsupported subclaim, so that $\mathbb{E}[M_t(c) \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] \leq M_{t-1}(c)$, whatever happened in earlier rounds. By Ville's inequality \cite{ramdas2023game}, the probability that $M_t(c)$ ever reaches $1/\alpha$ is then at most $\alpha$, regardless of the number of rounds and of the round at which the system stops. For a supported subclaim, on the other hand, the new passages typically score above $\bar{p}_{\mathrm{null}}$, and $M_t(c)$ grows multiplicatively over the rounds.

The constant $\bar{p}_{\mathrm{null}}$ is estimated from a pilot set $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{pilot}}$ of $m$ labelled queries, which we refer to as pilot queries. The pilot queries are processed by the same loop as any other query, and $\bar{p}_{\mathrm{null}}$ is set to an upper confidence bound on the mean of $p_t(c)$ over their unsupported subclaims, so that \eqref{eq:null_bound} holds with high probability over the pilot set. Note that the pilot set only estimates the average of $p_t(c)$ over the histories of the pilot queries, whereas \eqref{eq:null_bound} must hold given each history. It is important to note that that the pilot set must be kept separate from the $n$ calibration queries $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}$ of the final filtering step. For the guarantees at the final filtering step to hold at test time, $\bar{p}_{\mathrm{null}}$ and thus the decision rule must have been already fixed so that test-time and calibration time queries receive the same treatment by the final filtering step.

The decision block certifies the subclaims at the full level $\alpha$, following fixed-sequence testing (FST) \cite{bauer1991multiple}, in which hypotheses are tested in an order fixed in advance and a hypothesis is only rejected once all hypotheses before it have been rejected. Accordingly, a subclaim is only certified once every subclaim before it in the order $c_1, c_2, \ldots$ has been certified. Let $j_t$ be the position of the first subclaim whose e-process has not yet reached $1/\alpha$ by round $t$, where a subclaim that has been removed or rewritten counts as not certified. The certified subclaims are then
\begin{equation}
  k_t = \bigl\{ c_j : j < j_t \bigr\}, \qquad j_t = \min\Bigl\{ j : \max_{s \leq t} M_s(c_j) < 1/\alpha, \text{ or } c_j \text{ has been removed or rewritten} \Bigr\},
  \label{eq:certify}
\end{equation}
and every subclaim that is neither certified nor removed is reworked, i.e., $w_t = \hat{y}_t \setminus (k_t \cup z_t)$. As Ville's inequality bounds the probability that $M_t(c)$ ever reaches $1/\alpha$, a certified subclaim stays certified even if its e-process decreases later, which agrees with the subclaims of $k_t$ being kept unchanged by the revision block. All subclaims of $w_t$ accumulate evidence in parallel, so that a subclaim that waits behind an earlier subclaim may already exceed $1/\alpha$ when that subclaim is certified. Note that a single factor in \eqref{eq:betting} is at most $1/\bar{p}_{\mathrm{null}}$, so that, unless $\bar{p}_{\mathrm{null}} \leq \alpha$, a subclaim is only certified after several rounds in which strongly entailing passages are found for it. In the example, $c^{\star}_1$ is entailed by the Jaws article, so that $M_0(c^{\star}_1)$ increases and further passages about the film are retrieved for it, whereas no passage of $D_0$ entails $c^{\star}_2$, so that $M_0(c^{\star}_2) = 1$ and $c^{\star}_2$ is reworked.

The decision block stops at the round
\begin{equation}
  t^* = \min\bigl\{ t : w_t = \emptyset, \ \text{or} \ D_{t+1} = D_t, \ \text{or} \ t = T \bigr\},
  \label{eq:stop}
\end{equation}
that is, when every subclaim has been certified or removed, when the rework returns no new passages, so that no further evidence can be gained, or when the budget of $T$ rounds is used up. By Ville's inequality, any stopping rule preserves the validity of the e-processes, and \eqref{eq:stop} only determines how much evidence is collected.

\subsection{Query Generation}\label{subsec:query}

For each subclaim $c \in w_t$, an LLM writes the retrieval query $q_t(c)$ from the query $x$, the subclaim $c$ and the evidence $D_t$, so that the query can make use of a bridge entity identified in earlier rounds, and the returned passages are added to the evidence store as in \eqref{eq:store}. In the example, $c^{\star}_2$ yields a query such as ``Steven Spielberg birthplace'', which retrieves his biography. As a simpler alternative, the subclaim $c$ itself can be used as the retrieval query. Unlike in multi-hop C-RAG, whose queries are driven by the query $x$ as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:intro}, the queries of AC-RAG are written from the subclaims that are not yet certified.

\subsection{Subclaim Revision}\label{subsec:revision}

The revision block keeps the certified subclaims $k_t$ unchanged, and, for each subclaim $c \in w_t$, either leaves $c$ unchanged or rewrites it given the new evidence. It may also add new subclaims that the new evidence supports. A rewritten or added subclaim is a new hypothesis, and it is placed after the existing subclaims in the order, with an e-process that starts at $1$. A refinement of a certified subclaim, such as the month of an event whose year is already certified, is also added as a new subclaim, so that the certified subclaim keeps its status. In the example, $c^{\star}_2$ needs no rewriting, as Spielberg's biography entails it as it stands, so that $c^{\star}_2$ keeps its e-process, which grows with the new evidence and allows $c^{\star}_2$ to be certified once $c^{\star}_1$ is.

Under FST, a subclaim that is rewritten counts as not certified at its position, by \eqref{eq:certify}. Revisions therefore do not help the subclaims after this position to be certified by the e-processes, but they can still increase the number of supported subclaims that pass the final filtering step.

\subsection{Final Filtering Step}\label{subsec:final}

The returned set can be obtained in two ways, which we state side by side.

The first is to return the certified subclaims, $\hat{y} = k_{t^*}$. Let $c^*$ be the first unsupported subclaim in the order. Any error in \eqref{eq:cf_seq} requires certifying $c^*$, since every later subclaim is only certified after it, and this in turn requires $M_t(c^*) \geq 1/\alpha$ at some round. By Ville's inequality, this has probability at most $\alpha$, which gives \eqref{eq:cf_seq} without dividing $\alpha$ among the subclaims. The bound also holds when the evidence for $c^*$ was collected before $c^*$ came under test. As new subclaims are placed after the existing ones, $c^*$ is determined at the round at which it is created, which is also the round at which its e-process starts. This guarantee relies on \eqref{eq:null_bound}, and so holds with high probability over the pilot set.

The second is to apply the filter \eqref{eq:filter_set} to the remaining subclaims, $\hat{y} = F_{\hat{\tau}}(\hat{y}_{t^*})$, with the entailment score $s^{\mathrm{ent}}_{t^*}(c \mid x, D_{t^*})$ of \eqref{eq:nli_score}, where $\hat{\tau}$ is chosen as in CF, but from calibration queries that have each been processed by the whole AC-RAG loop. As long as the loop is fixed in advance and is the same at calibration and test time, the test query and the calibration queries remain exchangeable, and \eqref{eq:cf_seq} holds regardless of \eqref{eq:null_bound} and of how many subclaims the loop recovers. With $T = 0$, this reduces to C-RAG with the entailment score $s^{\mathrm{ent}}_0$ of \eqref{eq:nli_score}.
