\section{Active C-RAG}\label{sec:approach}

In this section we describe our implementation of the schema given in Section~\ref{sec:prob_form}, for each of the system components in Figure~\ref{fig:system}.

\subsection{Base Pipeline}\label{subsec:base}

AC-RAG uses round $0$ of (multi-hop) C-RAG, unchanged, i.e., the initial retrieval, the LLM $G$ and the subclaim extraction, so that with $T = 0$, the system differs from these methods only in the subclaim scorer.

\subsection{Subclaim Scorer}\label{subsec:scorer}

In practice, the retrieved documents are short passages, such as chunks of an article or single sentences, and we refer to them as passages. The score \eqref{eq:crag_score} has two key issues when the answer to a query relies on a bridge entity. Firstly, the cosine similarity $\mathrm{sim}(c, d)$ measures how close a subclaim and a passage are in topic, not whether the passage supports the subclaim. Secondly, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:intro}, the factor $\mathrm{sim}(x, d)$ penalises passages about the bridge entity, as these are not very similar to the query. Hence, the score of $c^{\star}_2$ would remain low even if Spielberg's biography were retrieved. To score whether the evidence supports a subclaim, we therefore replace \eqref{eq:crag_score} by a natural language inference (NLI) model. For a passage $d$ and a subclaim $c$, the NLI model returns the probabilities $p_{\mathrm{ent}}(d, c)$, $p_{\mathrm{con}}(d, c)$ and $p_{\mathrm{neu}}(d, c)$ that $d$ entails $c$, contradicts $c$, or neither, which sum to $1$. The scorer then assigns two scores to each subclaim $c \in \hat{y}_t$,
\begin{equation}
  s^{\mathrm{ent}}_t(c \mid x, D_t) = \max_{d \in D_t} p_{\mathrm{ent}}(d, c), \qquad s^{\mathrm{con}}_t(c \mid x, D_t) = \max_{d \in D_t} p_{\mathrm{con}}(d, c),
  \label{eq:nli_score}
\end{equation}
such that $s_t$ now contains several scores for each subclaim. The entailment score $s^{\mathrm{ent}}_t$ is high only if some passage entails $c$, and it does not depend on how the query $x$ is worded. Spielberg's biography, for instance, clearly entails $c^{\star}_2$, although it is not similar to $x$. The contradiction score $s^{\mathrm{con}}_t$ is high if some passage contradicts $c$, and it is only used by the decision block to remove subclaims. Entailment-based scorers have also been found to match or outperform LLM confidence scores for conformal factuality in RAG, at a far lower computational cost \cite{chen2026conformal}.

The scorer also returns the evidence that each round adds for each subclaim. For a subclaim $c$, let $N_t(c) \subseteq D_t$ denote the passages of $D_t$ that have not been scored against $c$ in earlier rounds. The new evidence for $c$ at round $t$ is then the best of these passages,
\begin{equation}
  p_t(c) = \max_{d \in N_t(c)} p_{\mathrm{ent}}(d, c),
  \label{eq:new_score}
\end{equation}
which is only defined when $N_t(c) \neq \emptyset$. A passage therefore contributes to $p_t(c)$ at most once for each subclaim, including a passage that was retrieved for a different subclaim.

\subsection{Decision Block}\label{subsec:decision}

% \tk{Why not e-scores: they compare a subclaim's score with those of false subclaims from the pilot set, computed once at round $0$. It is therefore only valid for a single comparison at round $0$. After rework, the subclaim is scored on evidence retrieved specifically for it, so the comparison no longer holds, and checking it again at every round inflates the error. Calibrating a fresh e-score on a pilot set at each round wouldn't resolve this either, since multiplying them requires each one to be valid given the history of the query, whereas a conformal e-score is only valid on average over queries. So instead we can use betting, in which each round is a new bet that is fair given everything observed before, and the bets are multiplied into an e-process.}

Taking the null hypothesis $H_c$ for each subclaim $c$ to be that $c$ is unsupported, i.e., $a(c \mid x) = 0$, the decision block accumulates evidence against $H_c$ over the rounds. Its decisions only steer the loop, i.e., they determine which subclaims are kept, reworked or removed, and when to stop, whereas the guarantee \eqref{eq:cf_seq} is obtained by the final filtering step.

The decision block first removes the subclaims that the evidence contradicts. To this end, a subclaim is removed when at least one passage contradicts it and no passage entails it, which, in terms of the scores \eqref{eq:nli_score}, gives
\begin{equation}
  z_t = \bigl\{ c \in \hat{y}_t \setminus k_{t-1} : s^{\mathrm{con}}_t(c \mid x, D_t) > 1/2 \ \text{and} \ s^{\mathrm{ent}}_t(c \mid x, D_t) \leq 1/2 \bigr\},
  \label{eq:removal}
\end{equation}
with $k_{t-1} = \emptyset$ for $t = 0$. Since the three probabilities of the NLI model sum to $1$, a probability above $1/2$ is the largest of the three, so that a passage with $p_{\mathrm{con}}(d, c) > 1/2$ contradicts $c$, and $s^{\mathrm{ent}}_t(c \mid x, D_t) \leq 1/2$ means that no passage entails $c$ with a probability above $1/2$. We note that removal requires no calibration, as a removed subclaim is never returned, and thus cannot lead to an error in \eqref{eq:cf_seq}.

For the remaining subclaims, the decision block accumulates evidence over the rounds with an e-process \cite{ramdas2023game}, which is built from a bound on how well the new evidence for an unsupported subclaim scores. Specifically, we require a constant $\bar{p}_{\mathrm{null}} \in (0, 1)$ such that, for every subclaim $c$ with $a(c \mid x) = 0$ and every round $t$ at which $p_t(c)$ is defined,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}\bigl[\, p_t(c) \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1} \,\bigr] \leq \bar{p}_{\mathrm{null}},
  \label{eq:null_bound}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$ denotes everything observed up to round $t-1$. In other words, $\bar{p}_{\mathrm{null}}$ is the level up to which, on average, the NLI model is fooled by the new evidence for an unsupported subclaim. For each subclaim $c$, the e-process $M_t(c)$ starts at $1$ at the round at which $c$ is created, and at every round at which $p_t(c)$ is defined, it is updated as
\begin{equation}
  M_t(c) = M_{t-1}(c) \bigl(1 + \lambda_t(c) \bigl(p_t(c) - \bar{p}_{\mathrm{null}}\bigr)\bigr),
  \label{eq:betting}
\end{equation}
while it is left unchanged at the other rounds. The stake $\lambda_t(c) \in [0, 1/\bar{p}_{\mathrm{null}}]$ is chosen from $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$, for instance as a constant or adaptively as in \cite{waudbysmith2023betting}, and its upper limit keeps $M_t(c)$ non-negative. Under \eqref{eq:null_bound}, each factor in \eqref{eq:betting} has expectation at most $1$ given $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$ for an unsupported subclaim, so that $\mathbb{E}[M_t(c) \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] \leq M_{t-1}(c)$, whatever happened in earlier rounds. By Ville's inequality \cite{ramdas2023game}, the probability that $M_t(c)$ ever reaches $1/\alpha$ is then at most $\alpha$, regardless of the number of rounds and of the round at which the system stops. For a supported subclaim, on the other hand, the new passages typically score above $\bar{p}_{\mathrm{null}}$, and $M_t(c)$ grows multiplicatively over the rounds. 
% Note that \eqref{eq:null_bound} is a design assumption, which makes $M_t(c)$ a meaningful measure of the evidence for $c$, and not a condition for the guarantee \eqref{eq:cf_seq}, which holds whether or not \eqref{eq:null_bound} holds.

The constant $\bar{p}_{\mathrm{null}}$ is estimated from a pilot set $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{pilot}}$ of $m$ labelled queries, which we refer to as pilot queries. The pilot queries are processed by the same loop as any other query, and $\bar{p}_{\mathrm{null}}$ is set to an upper confidence bound on the mean of $p_t(c)$ over their unsupported subclaims, so that \eqref{eq:null_bound} holds with high probability over the pilot set. Note that the pilot set only estimates the average of $p_t(c)$ over the histories of the pilot queries, whereas \eqref{eq:null_bound} is stated given each history. It is important to note that that the pilot set must be kept separate from the $n$ calibration queries $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}$ of the final filtering step. For the guarantees at the final filtering step to hold at test time, $\bar{p}_{\mathrm{null}}$ and thus the decision rule must have been already fixed so that test-time and calibration time queries receive the same treatment by the final filtering step.

A subclaim is kept once its e-process has reached $1/\alpha_{\mathrm{keep}}$, i.e.,
\begin{equation}
  k_t = k_{t-1} \cup \bigl\{ c \in \hat{y}_t \setminus (k_{t-1} \cup z_t) : M_t(c) \geq 1/\alpha_{\mathrm{keep}} \bigr\},
  \label{eq:keep}
\end{equation}
and every subclaim that is neither kept nor removed is reworked, i.e., $w_t = \hat{y}_t \setminus (k_t \cup z_t)$. The tolerance $\alpha_{\mathrm{keep}}$ is fixed on the pilot set and only steers the loop, so that it need not equal the level $\alpha$ of \eqref{eq:cf_seq}. A kept subclaim is no longer reworked, which agrees with the subclaims of $k_t$ being kept unchanged by the revision block, and protects a subclaim that is already well supported from being reworded. All subclaims of $w_t$ accumulate evidence in parallel. Note that a single factor in \eqref{eq:betting} is at most $1/\bar{p}_{\mathrm{null}}$, so that, unless $\bar{p}_{\mathrm{null}} \leq \alpha_{\mathrm{keep}}$, a subclaim is only kept after several rounds in which strongly entailing passages are found for it. In the example, $c^{\star}_1$ is entailed by the Jaws article, so that $M_0(c^{\star}_1)$ increases and further passages about the film are retrieved for it, whereas no passage of $D_0$ entails $c^{\star}_2$, so that $M_0(c^{\star}_2) = 1$ and $c^{\star}_2$ is reworked.

The decision block stops at the round
\begin{equation}
  t^* = \min\bigl\{ t : w_t = \emptyset, \ \text{or} \ D_{t+1} = D_t, \ \text{or} \ t = T \bigr\},
  \label{eq:stop}
\end{equation}
that is, when every subclaim has been kept or removed, when the rework returns no new passages, so that no further evidence can be gained, or when the budget of $T$ rounds is used up. As the threshold of the final filtering step is calibrated on queries that follow the same stopping rule, any stopping rule preserves the guarantee \eqref{eq:cf_seq}, and \eqref{eq:stop} only determines how much evidence is collected.

\subsection{Query Generation}\label{subsec:query}

For each subclaim $c \in w_t$, an LLM writes the retrieval query $q_t(c)$ from the query $x$, the subclaim $c$ and the evidence $D_t$, so that the query can make use of a bridge entity identified in earlier rounds, and the returned passages are added to the evidence store as in \eqref{eq:store}. In the example, $c^{\star}_2$ yields a query such as ``Steven Spielberg birthplace'', which retrieves his biography. As a simpler alternative, the subclaim $c$ itself can be used as the retrieval query. Unlike in multi-hop C-RAG, whose queries are rewritten from the query $x$ and the documents retrieved so far, as described in Section~\ref{sec:background}, the queries of AC-RAG are written from the subclaims that are not yet kept.

\subsection{Subclaim Revision}\label{subsec:revision}

The revision block keeps the subclaims of $k_t$ unchanged, and, for each subclaim $c \in w_t$, either leaves $c$ unchanged or rewrites it given the new evidence. It may also add new subclaims that the new evidence supports. A rewritten or added subclaim is a new hypothesis, with an e-process that starts at $1$. A refinement of a kept subclaim, such as the month of an event whose year is already kept, is also added as a new subclaim, so that the kept subclaim keeps its status. In the example, $c^{\star}_2$ needs no rewriting, as Spielberg's biography entails it as it stands, so that $c^{\star}_2$ keeps its e-process, which grows with the new evidence until $c^{\star}_2$ is kept. \tk{as we discussed, it might be worth ignoring this for now}

\subsection{Final Filtering Step}\label{subsec:final}

The final filtering step applies the filter \eqref{eq:filter_set} to the remaining subclaims, with the e-process at the stopping round as the score, i.e.,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{y} = F_{\hat{\tau}}(\hat{y}_{t^*}) = \bigl\{ c \in \hat{y}_{t^*} : M_{t^*}(c) \geq \hat{\tau} \bigr\},
  \label{eq:final_filter}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\tau}$ is chosen as in CF, but from calibration queries that have each been processed by the whole AC-RAG loop. As long as the loop is fixed in advance and is the same at calibration and test time, the test query and the calibration queries remain exchangeable, and \eqref{eq:cf_seq} holds regardless of \eqref{eq:null_bound}, of the stopping rule, and of how many subclaims the loop recovers.

The e-process is used as the score of the final filtering step, rather than the entailment score $s^{\mathrm{ent}}_{t^*}(c \mid x, D_{t^*})$ of \eqref{eq:nli_score}, since $s^{\mathrm{ent}}_{t^*}$ is a maximum over all passages. An unsupported subclaim that is reworked over many rounds therefore has many chances to find a passage that the NLI model mistakes for support, which raises its entailment score, and with it the threshold $\hat{\tau}$. Its e-process, on the other hand, decreases at every round in which the new evidence is weak. If \eqref{eq:null_bound} holds, $\hat{\tau}$ is close to $1/\alpha$, whereas otherwise the calibration raises $\hat{\tau}$ accordingly, so that a violation of \eqref{eq:null_bound} reduces the usefulness \eqref{eq:usefulness}, but not the guarantee \eqref{eq:cf_seq}. With $T = 0$ and a constant stake, $M_0(c)$ is an increasing function of $s^{\mathrm{ent}}_0(c \mid x, D_0)$, so that the final filtering step reduces to C-RAG with the entailment score of \eqref{eq:nli_score}.

This would give an e-value version of the final filtering step, which allows $\alpha$ to be chosen after the scores have been seen. They remain unsuitable inside the loop, as they are only valid for a single comparison.}

\section{Questions for Discussion}

It would be great to get your input on some of these
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item How should $\bar{p}_{\mathrm{null}}$ be estimated? Since \eqref{eq:null_bound} only affects how well the loop is steered, is an upper confidence bound on the mean over the pilot queries sufficient, or should we use the robust bet of~\cite{shaer2026testing}?
  \item Should the e-process bet once per round on the best new passage, as in \eqref{eq:new_score}, or once per passage? Betting once per passage keeps supported subclaims in fewer rounds, but makes \eqref{eq:null_bound} less plausible when several passages come from the same article.
  \item Is $M_{t^*}(c)$ the right score for the final filtering step \eqref{eq:final_filter}, or should it be combined with the entailment score $s^{\mathrm{ent}}_{t^*}$?
  \item Should the kept set $k_{t^*}$ carry a guarantee of its own, as outlined in Section~\ref{subsec:decision}, or is the guarantee of the final filtering step sufficient?
\end{enumerate}
