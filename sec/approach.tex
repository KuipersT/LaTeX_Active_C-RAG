\section{Active C-RAG}\label{sec:approach}

In this section we introduce Active C-RAG (AC-RAG), which follows the general block diagram in Figure~\ref{fig:system}, incorporating iterative evidence retrieval and subclaim editing under the requirement \eqref{eq:cf}. AC-RAG obtains the guarantee \eqref{eq:cf} from the final filtering step, and selects the hyperparameters of the decision block so as to control the risk \eqref{eq:risk} of the returned set.

\subsection{Subclaim Scorer}\label{subsec:scorer}

We start by adapting the subclaim score \eqref{eq:crag_score} to settings like Figure~\ref{fig:system}, that retrieve evidence not only based on the query $x$ but also based on intermediate subclaims. To this end, we leverage a natural language inference (NLI) model. For a passage $d$ and a subclaim $c$, the NLI model \tk{REFS}, which returns the probabilities $p_{\mathrm{ent}}(d, c)$, $p_{\mathrm{con}}(d, c)$ and $p_{\mathrm{neu}}(d, c)$ that document $d$ entails, contradicts, or is neutral about subclaim $c$, with $p_{\mathrm{ent}}(\cdot) + p_{\mathrm{con}}(\cdot) + p_{\mathrm{neu}}(\cdot) = 1$. The scorer then assigns two scores $s_t = [s^{\mathrm{ent}}_{t}, s^{\mathrm{con}}_{t}]$ to each subclaim $c \in \hat{y}_t$, namely
\begin{equation}
  s^{\mathrm{ent}}_t(c \mid x, D_t) = \max_{d \in D_t} p_{\mathrm{ent}}(d, c) \quad \text{and} \quad s^{\mathrm{con}}_t(c \mid x, D_t) = \max_{d \in D_t} p_{\mathrm{con}}(d, c).
  \label{eq:nli_score}
\end{equation}
These scores depend only on whether some document $d \in D_t$ entails or contradicts $c$, and they do not depend on how the query is worded, making it easier to extract evidence from later rounds. Entailment-based scorers have also been found to match or outperform LLM confidence scores for conformal factuality in RAG, at a far lower computational cost \cite{chen2026conformal}.

\subsection{Decision Block}\label{subsec:decision}

The decision block partitions the subclaims based on the scores \eqref{eq:nli_score}, using two thresholds $\lambda_{\mathrm{ent}}$ and $\lambda_{\mathrm{con}}$ in $[0, 1]$. A subclaim that some passage entails with a probability of at least $\lambda_{\mathrm{ent}}$ is kept, a subclaim that some passage contradicts with a probability of at least $\lambda_{\mathrm{con}}$, while no passage entails it with a probability of at least $\lambda_{\mathrm{ent}}$, is removed, and the remaining subclaims are reworked, i.e.,
\begin{align}
  k_t &= k_{t-1} \cup \bigl\{ c \in \hat{y}_t \setminus k_{t-1} : s^{\mathrm{ent}}_t(c \mid x, D_t) \geq \lambda_{\mathrm{ent}} \bigr\}, \label{eq:keep} \\
  z_t &= \bigl\{ c \in \hat{y}_t \setminus k_{t-1} : s^{\mathrm{con}}_t(c \mid x, D_t) \geq \lambda_{\mathrm{con}} \ \text{and} \ s^{\mathrm{ent}}_t(c \mid x, D_t) < \lambda_{\mathrm{ent}} \bigr\}, \label{eq:removal} \\
  w_t &= \hat{y}_t \setminus (k_t \cup z_t), \label{eq:rework}
\end{align}
with $k_{-1} = \emptyset$. The threshold $\lambda_{\mathrm{ent}}$ determines how much evidence a subclaim needs before it is no longer reworked, and $\lambda_{\mathrm{con}}$ how strongly the evidence must contradict a subclaim before it is removed. For instance, since the three probabilities of the NLI model sum to $1$, a threshold $\lambda_{\mathrm{con}} > 1/2$ only removes a subclaim if contradiction is the most likely relation for some passage. A kept subclaim is no longer reworked, which agrees with the subclaims of $k_t$ being kept unchanged by the revision block, and protects a subclaim that is already well supported from being reworded. In the example, $c^{\star}_1$ is entailed by the Jaws article and is kept, whereas no passage of $D_0$ entails $c^{\star}_2$, which is therefore reworked.

The decision block stops at the round
\begin{equation}
  t^* = \min\bigl\{ t : w_t = \emptyset, \ \text{or} \ |k_t| \geq \gamma\, |\hat{y}_t|, \ \text{or} \ D_{t+1} = D_t, \ \text{or} \ t = T \bigr\},
  \label{eq:stop}
\end{equation}
that is, when no subclaim remains to be reworked, when a fraction $\gamma \in (0, 1]$ of the current subclaims has been kept, when the rework returns no new passages, or when the budget of $T$ rounds is used up. The fraction $\gamma$ allows the system to stop once enough of the answer is supported, rather than reworking every remaining subclaim. We collect the hyperparameters of the decision block in $\lambda = (\lambda_{\mathrm{ent}}, \lambda_{\mathrm{con}}, \gamma)$.

\subsection{Query Generation}\label{subsec:query}

For each subclaim $c \in w_t$, an LLM writes the retrieval query $q_t(c)$ from the query $x$, the subclaim $c$ and the evidence $D_t$, so that the query can make use of a bridge entity identified in earlier rounds, and the returned passages are added to the evidence store as in \eqref{eq:store}, so that the query $q_t(x, w_t, D_t)$ of Section~\ref{sec:prob_form} consists of one query for each subclaim. In the example, $c^{\star}_2$ yields a query such as ``Steven Spielberg birthplace'', which retrieves his biography. As a simpler alternative, the subclaim $c$ itself can be used as the retrieval query. Unlike in multi-hop C-RAG, whose retrieval hops are driven by the query $x$, the queries of AC-RAG are written from the subclaims that are not yet kept.

\subsection{Subclaim Revision}\label{subsec:revision}

The revision block keeps the subclaims of $k_t$ unchanged, and, for each subclaim $c \in w_t$, either leaves $c$ unchanged or rewrites it given the new evidence. It may also add new subclaims that the new evidence supports. A rewritten or added subclaim is scored again at the next round, as any other subclaim. A refinement of a kept subclaim, such as the month of an event whose year is already kept, is also added as a new subclaim, so that the kept subclaim keeps its status. In the example, $c^{\star}_2$ needs no rewriting, as Spielberg's biography entails it as it stands, so that its entailment score increases with the new evidence, until $c^{\star}_2$ is kept. \tk{as we discussed, it might be worth ignoring this for now}

\subsection{Final Filtering Step}\label{subsec:final}

Since the decisions of the loop are based on scores that are not calibrated, the guarantee \eqref{eq:cf} is obtained by the final filtering step. For hyperparameters $\lambda$, the final filtering block applies the filter \eqref{eq:filter_set} to the remaining subclaims, with the entailment score at the stopping round as the score, i.e.,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{y}_{\lambda}(x) = \bigl\{ c \in \hat{y}_{t^*} : s^{\mathrm{ent}}_{t^*}(c \mid x, D_{t^*}) \geq \hat{\tau}_{\lambda} \bigr\},
  \label{eq:final_cf}
\end{equation}
where the threshold $\hat{\tau}_{\lambda}$ is chosen as in CF, but from calibration queries that have each been processed by the loop with the same hyperparameters $\lambda$. As the calibration queries and the new query receive the same treatment, they remain exchangeable, and \eqref{eq:cf} holds for any fixed $\lambda$, regardless of the stopping rule and of the number of rounds. With $T = 0$, AC-RAG reduces to C-RAG with the entailment score of \eqref{eq:nli_score}.

The returned sets have two properties that are useful for selecting $\lambda$. Firstly, for a given query and given hyperparameters, the returned sets are nested in the threshold, i.e., a larger threshold returns a subset of the subclaims returned by a smaller one. Secondly, the hyperparameters only affect the returned set through the subclaims and the evidence that reach the final filtering step, and through the threshold $\hat{\tau}_{\lambda}$, which is calibrated separately for each $\lambda$. Changing $\lambda$ therefore does not affect the guarantee \eqref{eq:cf}, but it changes how large and how informative the returned set is.

\subsection{Selecting the Hyperparameters}\label{subsec:ltt}

The guarantee \eqref{eq:cf} holds for any $\lambda$, but the quality of the returned set depends strongly on $\lambda$. For instance, a low threshold $\lambda_{\mathrm{ent}}$ stops reworking subclaims early, which leaves weakly supported subclaims to be removed by the final filtering step, whereas a high threshold keeps reworking them, at the cost of more rounds. We therefore select $\lambda$ so as to control the risk \eqref{eq:risk} of a loss $L$ that measures the quality of the returned set. Examples include the indicator that the returned set contains fewer than a given number of subclaims, a measure of the redundancy among the returned subclaims, and the quality of the final answer obtained by merging the returned subclaims, as judged against the reference $y^*$.

To this end, we use the Learn-then-Test (LTT) framework~\cite{angelopoulos2021learn}, which turns the selection of hyperparameters into a multiple hypothesis testing problem. For each $\lambda$ in a finite grid $\Lambda$, LTT tests the null hypothesis $H_{\lambda}$ that $R(\lambda) > \varepsilon$, i.e., that $\lambda$ does not achieve the target level $\varepsilon$. A p-value for $H_{\lambda}$ is computed from the losses of $\lambda$ on a set of labelled queries, e.g., by means of a concentration inequality, and a procedure that controls the family-wise error rate at level $\delta$, e.g., the Bonferroni correction, determines the set $\hat{\Lambda}$ of hyperparameters whose null hypotheses are rejected. With probability at least $1 - \delta$ over the labelled queries, every $\lambda \in \hat{\Lambda}$ then satisfies $R(\lambda) \leq \varepsilon$, so that any of them can be used, e.g., the one that requires the fewest rounds on average. If the risk is monotone along some direction of the grid, fixed-sequence testing can be used instead of the Bonferroni correction, which avoids dividing $\delta$ among the hyperparameters.

LTT requires labelled queries on which the loss of every $\lambda \in \Lambda$ can be evaluated, i.e., queries with references $y^*$ that allow both the subclaims to be labelled and the quality of the returned set to be assessed. As the hyperparameters of AC-RAG are intended to be used across tasks, these queries are drawn from multiple domains, e.g., several question answering datasets, and the risk \eqref{eq:risk} is then an average over the same mixture of domains. A guarantee for a specific domain requires labelled queries from that domain.

The labelled queries used by LTT and the calibration queries used by the final filtering step can be obtained in two ways. In the first, they form two disjoint sets. The threshold $\hat{\tau}_{\lambda}$ is computed on the calibration queries, and LTT evaluates the losses of the resulting returned sets on its own queries, so that the guarantee of LTT holds given the calibration queries. The guarantee \eqref{eq:cf} is exact for any $\lambda$ that is fixed in advance, but, as the selected $\lambda$ depends on the thresholds $\hat{\tau}_{\lambda}$, the calibration queries should be refreshed after $\lambda$ has been selected, so that \eqref{eq:cf} also holds for the selected $\lambda$. In the second, the same labelled queries are used for both steps, by including the threshold of the final filtering step among the hyperparameters, and letting LTT control both the risk \eqref{eq:risk} and the probability that the returned set contains an unsupported subclaim. This uses the labelled queries more efficiently and gives a single guarantee for both risks, but it replaces the guarantee \eqref{eq:cf} of CF, which holds on average over the calibration queries, by one that holds with probability at least $1 - \delta$ over them. \tk{For discussion: which of the two ways should we follow?}
